## 2. Long-video narration script (12 scenes)

**1.** On October twenty-ninth, thieves pulled about six point three billion dollars out of Binance, the world's biggest crypto exchange. According to blockchain trackers, most of it was gone in about twenty minutes. It's the largest crypto theft on record.
[Hacker silhouette at a glowing laptop; a giant "$6.3B" counter racing down toward zero next to a 20-minute stopwatch.]

**2.** But here's the strange part. Binance says its customers won't lose a single cent. Six billion dollars, gone, and nobody's balance goes down? So who actually got robbed? And why did almost half of the loot freeze in place, as if someone had pressed pause?
[Confused cartoon investor staring at a phone showing an unchanged balance; a sack of coins suspended mid-air, covered in frost.]

**3.** Quick background. Binance launched in 2017 and grew into the largest crypto exchange by daily trading volume. It's where a lot of people buy bitcoin. And here's the key detail: many of them never take it home.
[Busy cartoon exchange lobby with a huge neon sign; shoppers walking out with receipts, not coins.]

**4.** Coin Check Math. Keeping crypto on an exchange is like a coat check. You hand over your coat and get a little ticket. Your coat goes in the back, and the coat check holds the key to that room. With crypto, that key is a secret code called a private key, and whoever holds it can move the coins. On an exchange, the exchange holds it. Your balance is your ticket.
[COIN CHECK MATH stamp. Mascot Dummy behind a coat-check counter swapping a gold coin for a paper ticket; a key ring hangs on his belt.]

**5.** Now, a coat check keeps a few coats right by the door so it can hand them back fast. Exchanges do the same thing with "hot wallets", which are online so withdrawals go out quickly. According to reports, that is exactly where the money left from.
[Cartoon vault split in two: a deep locked back room, and a small open rack by the front door labeled "HOT" with a few flames.]

**6.** So how did they get in? Binance's early investigation pointed to one engineer's laptop, which may have been hacked. From there, the attacker reportedly sent withdrawal orders the system treated as real. According to analytics firm T-R-M Labs, the money flowed out across ten different blockchains. Binance says it found no evidence its private keys leaked.
[Single laptop with a sneaky green worm; ten pipes fan out from the vault, each spraying coins.]

**7.** Binance slammed the brakes and froze withdrawals. For scale: the previous record crypto theft, at the exchange Bybit in early 2025, was about one and a half billion dollars. This one was more than four times bigger.
[Cartoon CEO yanking a giant red emergency lever; side-by-side money stacks, a small one labeled 2025 and a towering one labeled 2026.]

**8.** Then came the twist. A big chunk of the loot was in stablecoins, digital dollars like U-S-D-T from Tether and U-S-D-C from Circle. Bitcoin has no boss. A stablecoin does, and its company can block a wallet address. Tether's own rules allow it. Tether says that within minutes, it froze more than one and a half billion dollars. According to reports, Circle froze about eight hundred million more. In total, Binance says, about three billion dollars was frozen.
[Hacker grinning at a bag of coins, then a giant cartoon hand presses a big blue FREEZE button; the bag turns into an ice block.]

**9.** And who was behind it? Analytics firms T-R-M Labs and Chain-AL-uh-sis pointed to TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit. Two days later, the FBI said the same. Binance was more careful. Its co-CEO said its investigation had not attributed responsibility to any individual. North Korea routinely denies hacking.
[Shadowy silhouette behind a "SUSPECT" magnifying glass; an FBI-style agent at a podium; a cautious CEO at a press conference.]

**10.** Now, Binance has an emergency fund for moments like this, called SAFU. It holds about one billion dollars. The theft was more than six times bigger. So Binance said it would cover the rest with its own company money. Three days after the theft, it said about ninety-five percent of the missing coins had been replaced, and withdrawals started reopening.
[Small piggy bank labeled "SAFU" next to a mountain-sized hole; Binance cartoon CEO pouring coins from a company safe to refill shelves.]

**11.** Customers rushed to the exit anyway. According to TechCrunch, net withdrawals hit three point six billion dollars in a few hours. That was about two percent of everything held there. By our math, Binance was holding roughly one hundred seventy billion dollars for its users. So the heist, the largest crypto theft on record, was only about four percent of the pile.
[Long cartoon line of investors holding tickets at the coat-check counter; a giant pile of coins with a small slice missing.]

**12.** So, who got robbed? Binance did. Customers were holding coat-check tickets, and the coat check chose to pay for the missing coats. Almost half the loot froze because digital dollars come with a company that can say "stop." If that made money make a little more sense, hit subscribe. We make money make sense.
[Dummy hands a ticket back with a real coat; the frozen loot block sits in a museum display case. End card.]

## 3. YouTube Shorts (independent scripts)

### Short A: "The Freeze Button" (about 97 words)
Hackers just stole billions in crypto, and then about a billion and a half of it froze. Here's how. On October twenty-ninth, thieves pulled about six point three billion dollars out of the exchange Binance. Part of the loot was U-S-D-T, a digital dollar made by a company called Tether. And unlike bitcoin, a stablecoin has a boss. Tether's rules let it blacklist a wallet address. Tether says that within minutes, it froze more than one and a half billion dollars. The thieves still had the coins. They just couldn't move them. Crypto, meet the pause button.
[Visuals: hacker celebrating, then a giant blue FREEZE button; the coin bag turns to ice.]

### Short B: "Who Pays When an Exchange Gets Robbed?" (about 96 words)
Binance just lost about six point three billion dollars to hackers, and says its customers won't lose a cent. How? When you leave crypto on an exchange, you don't really hold it. The exchange holds the secret keys, and you hold an IOU, like a coat-check ticket. So when the back room got robbed, Binance had a choice. Its emergency fund held about one billion dollars, way too small. So it said it would pay the rest with its own company money. Three days later, it said ninety-five percent was replaced. Lucky customers. Unlucky coat check.
[Visuals: worried investor with ticket, coat-check counter, tiny piggy bank vs giant hole, CEO refilling shelves.]
