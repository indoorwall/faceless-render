## 2. Long-video narration (12 scenes, 634 words)

**1.** In the year two thousand, two guys flew to Dallas and offered to sell their tiny company to Blockbuster for fifty million dollars. According to co-founder Marc Randolph, Blockbuster's CEO was struggling not to laugh. The tiny company was called Netflix.
[Cartoon 2000s boardroom in navy and yellow: CEO in a suit biting his lip, two nervous founders holding a red envelope; on-screen "$50M?"]

**2.** Today, Netflix is worth about two hundred fifty-five billion dollars. Blockbuster has exactly one store left, in Bend, Oregon. So how does a giant lose to a company it could have bought for pocket change?
[Split screen: giant glowing streaming TV vs. one lonely little blue-and-yellow store under a pine tree; question mark pops]

**3.** Blockbuster started in nineteen eighty-five as a single video shop in Dallas. By two thousand four, it had more than nine thousand stores and over eighty-four thousand employees. Friday night meant one thing: walk the aisles, grab a movie, grab some popcorn.
[1990s video store full of cartoon families, aisles of VHS boxes, a store-count counter spinning to "9,094"]

**4.** And when you brought the movie back late? You paid. According to reports, in two thousand alone, Blockbuster collected about eight hundred million dollars in late fees. That's roughly one dollar in every six it made. And sixteen times what Netflix was asking for.
[A forgetful cartoon guy at a counter, clerk ringing up a fee; cash register spits a long receipt; on-screen "$800M"]

**5.** Netflix, meanwhile, mailed DVDs in red envelopes. Pay a monthly price, keep the movie as long as you want, no late fees. Its pitch was simple: Blockbuster buys Netflix, Netflix runs the online side. Reed Hastings later recalled whispering to Randolph that Blockbuster was a thousand times their size. The answer was no.
[Red envelopes flying out of a tiny garage-style office into mailboxes; then the boardroom door closing on the two founders]

**6.** Why would anyone say no? Pizza Math time. Imagine Tony's Pizza. One dollar of every six Tony makes comes from fees on pizza boxes you forgot to return. Then a new shop opens next door: same pizza, no box fees, ever. Should Tony copy them? If he does, he kills his favorite money machine himself. That trap has a name: the innovator's dilemma. Your cash cow makes it painful to chase the future.
[PIZZA MATH stamp; mascot Dummy as Tony behind a counter hugging a stack of pizza boxes shaped like a cow; rival shop with a "NO FEES" sign]

**7.** But here's the twist. Blockbuster actually woke up. It launched Blockbuster Online in two thousand four, dropped late fees in two thousand five, and later let online customers swap DVDs in stores for free. It worked. In one quarter of two thousand seven, Netflix lost fifty-five thousand subscribers. Hastings said Blockbuster had thrown "everything but the kitchen sink" at them.
[Blockbuster store with a giant "NO LATE FEES" banner; Netflix founder sweating as a subscriber counter ticks down]

**8.** Then the dilemma bit back. Every free swap cost Blockbuster about two dollars, and the late-fee money was gone. Activist investor Carl Icahn, with three board seats, opposed the plan. In twenty-oh-seven, the CEO behind the plan stepped away. The new boss ended the free swaps and raised online prices. By twenty-ten, late fees were back.
[Board meeting turning red; an investor pointing at a shrinking money pile; CEO walking out with a cardboard box]

**9.** And while Blockbuster went backward, Netflix went forward. In January two thousand seven, it started streaming. No envelope. No store. No late fees, because there was nothing to return. Blockbuster, meanwhile, was carrying roughly a billion dollars in debt. Hastings said in two thousand nine: "If it hadn't been for their debt, they could have killed us."
[Cartoon family on a couch, movie beaming straight to a big TV; outside, a store sign flickering, a heavy chain labeled "DEBT"]

**10.** On September twenty-third, twenty-ten, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy, citing about nine hundred million dollars in debt. The next year, Dish Network bought what was left for three hundred twenty million. Stores closed one by one. In January twenty-twenty, the last one in New Zealand shut its doors, leaving a single franchise store in Bend, Oregon.
[Bankruptcy courtroom with a gavel; a world map where blue-and-yellow dots blink out until one is left]

**11.** Netflix did something Blockbuster couldn't. In September twenty twenty-three, it mailed its final red envelopes, shutting down its own DVD business on purpose. Today it has about three hundred twenty-five million paid memberships. And as of November thirteenth, twenty twenty-six, its market value was about two hundred fifty-five billion dollars. That's not cash in a vault. It's the price of all its shares. But it's about five thousand times what Blockbuster turned down.
[Last red envelope dropping into a mailbox with a little salute; stock counter racing to "$255B"; "x5,000" stamp]

**12.** So how did the giant lose? Blockbuster saw the future. It just looked like giving up hundreds of millions in late fees, and Netflix had no fees to lose. If that made money make a little more sense, hit subscribe. We make money make sense.
[Dummy the mascot in the empty Bend store, holding a rewound tape, shrugging at the camera; channel logo]

## 3. YouTube Shorts (independent scripts)

### Short A: "The $40 late fee" (87 words)
A forty-dollar late fee may have started a two-hundred-billion-dollar company. Reed Hastings has said a forty-dollar Blockbuster late bill inspired him to start Netflix. Back then, late fees were Blockbuster's secret money machine. According to reports, about eight hundred million dollars in two thousand alone. So Netflix did the opposite: one monthly price, no late fees. Blockbuster later had the chance to buy Netflix for fifty million dollars. It said no. Today Netflix is worth about two hundred fifty-five billion. Not bad for one overdue movie. Subscribe!

### Short B: "The last one standing" (89 words)
There used to be more than nine thousand Blockbusters. Now there's one. It's in Bend, Oregon. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in twenty-ten. Stores shut down across the world. In January twenty-twenty, the last one in New Zealand closed, and the little franchise store in Bend became the last Blockbuster on the planet. There's even a documentary about it. Meanwhile, Netflix, the company that made rental stores feel old, shut down its own DVD-by-mail business in twenty twenty-three. Which means the last Blockbuster outlasted Netflix's DVDs. Be kind. Rewind. Subscribe.

(Note for Short B: "Be kind. Rewind." is used as a generic VHS-era phrase, not presented as a Blockbuster slogan.)
