## 2. Long video narration (12 scenes, 645 words)

**1.** In January 2009, Zimbabwe's central bank rolled out a single banknote worth one hundred trillion dollars. That's a one with fourteen zeros. And according to Reuters, on the black market it was worth about thirty U.S. dollars.
[Visual: cartoon man in a Harare street holding up a giant 100-trillion note with a proud grin; a tiny price tag pops beside it: "≈ $30". He stops grinning.]

**2.** So here's a question almost everyone asks at some point. If a government can print money... why doesn't it just print a mountain of it and make everyone rich? Zimbabwe tried something close to that. So did Germany, a hundred years ago.
[Visual: Dummy the mascot at a chalkboard with a big "?" next to a cartoon printing press spitting out cash.]

**3.** Let's start with Germany in 1923. After World War One, the country owed huge payments to the winners. Then French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, its industrial heartland. Workers went on strike, and the government paid them... by printing more money.
[Visual: rewind counter spinning back to 1923; cartoon factory district with smokestacks gone quiet, soldiers at the gate, a printing press in Berlin running hot.]

**4.** According to historians, a loaf of bread in Berlin cost about one hundred sixty marks at the end of 1922. By late 1923, it cost around two hundred billion marks. By November, one U.S. dollar was worth more than four trillion marks. Paper money was so worthless that some people used it as wallpaper.
[Visual: baker flipping a price sign that keeps adding zeros until it falls off the edge; cut to a family calmly gluing banknotes on their living-room wall.]

**5.** Now jump to Zimbabwe in the 2000s. Land reform hurt the farms, so the country grew less food. At the same time, the government printed money to pay for a war in Congo and bigger salaries for soldiers and officials. Less stuff. More money. You can probably guess what happened.
[Visual: half-empty cartoon farm field on one side, a printing press working overtime on the other; a seesaw tipping hard toward the cash pile.]

**6.** By January 2009, according to Reuters, prices were doubling every day. People waited in line for hours at banks to withdraw barely enough for a loaf of bread. The central bank kept chopping zeros off the currency: three, then ten, then twelve more. In April 2009, Zimbabwe gave up and switched to foreign money, mostly U.S. dollars.
[Visual: long line outside a cartoon bank; a giant pair of scissors snipping zeros off a banknote; the Zimbabwe note waves goodbye as a U.S. dollar walks in.]

**7.** Time for some Pizza Math. This is a super simplified example, not a real economy. Picture a tiny town with ten pizzas and ten dollars. Each pizza costs one dollar. Now the mayor prints ten more dollars. Still only ten pizzas. Everyone has more cash, everyone wants pizza, and the price climbs to two dollars. Nobody got richer. Each dollar just got smaller.
[Visual: PIZZA MATH stamp; 10 pizzas on a counter, 10 dollar bills, "$1" sign; mayor cranks a mini printing press, bills double, sign flips to "$2"; customer's jaw drops.]

**8.** But here's the part people forget. If the town also bakes ten more pizzas, the price can stay at one dollar. Prices depend on how much money there is, and on how much stuff the economy actually makes. Zimbabwe got hit from both sides: more money, fewer pizzas.
[Visual: chef rolls in 10 extra pizzas, sign flips back to "$1"; then a split screen: Zimbabwe side with a giant cash pile and one sad pizza.]

**9.** Now, America. In 2020, the pandemic hit, and Washington sent out stimulus checks: twelve hundred dollars a person, then six hundred, then fourteen hundred in March 2021. Federal Reserve data shows the money supply jumped from about fifteen and a half trillion dollars in February 2020 to about twenty-one and a half trillion by the end of 2021. By our math, that's almost forty percent more, in under two years.
[Visual: family at the kitchen table opening a check envelope; counter ticking up from "$15.5T" to "$21.5T" over the Fed building.]

**10.** Meanwhile, the pizzas got stuck. The pandemic tangled up supply chains, and energy prices shot up. In June 2022, the Bureau of Labor Statistics said prices were up nine point one percent from a year earlier. That's the biggest jump since 1981. Gasoline alone was up almost sixty percent. One San Francisco Fed study estimated supply problems explained about half of that rise, and demand about a third.
[Visual: grocery store shelves with gaps; a gas pump whose numbers spin fast; shopper staring at a receipt that keeps unrolling. Single chart: CPI line peaking at "9.1%".]

**11.** Here's the twist: America was not Zimbabwe. Nine percent hurt, but prices weren't doubling every day. In March 2022, the Fed started raising interest rates to cool things down. And its goal was never zero inflation. The Fed aims for about two percent a year, low and steady enough that families can plan ahead.
[Visual: Fed chair character turning a big dial labeled "rates" up; thermometer cooling; Dummy holds a small sign "2% = normal".]

**12.** So why can't the government just print more money? Because money isn't wealth. Pizzas are. Print cash without making more stuff, and nobody gets richer: every dollar just buys less, and that's how you end up with a hundred-trillion-dollar bill worth about thirty bucks. If that made money make a little more sense, hit subscribe. We make money make sense.
[Visual: back to the man from scene 1, now holding the 100-trillion note next to a single pizza slice; Dummy winks and points at the subscribe button.]

## 3. YouTube Shorts (own narration, not cut from the long)

### Short 1: "Bread for two hundred billion" (97 words)
At the end of 1922, a loaf of bread in Berlin cost about a hundred and sixty marks. A year later? Around two hundred billion. Same bread. Same bakery.
So what changed? According to historians, Germany's government was paying striking workers by printing more and more money. The bread didn't get better. The money got worse.
By November 1923, one U.S. dollar was worth more than four trillion marks. Some people literally used banknotes as wallpaper.
The fix? A brand-new currency, with twelve zeros chopped off prices.
Turns out, the cheapest wallpaper is a broken money printer.
[Visual: baker's price sign gaining zeros, family gluing notes to a wall, giant scissors snipping twelve zeros.]

### Short 2: "Why does the Fed WANT prices to rise?" (97 words)
The Federal Reserve wants your groceries to get more expensive. On purpose. A little.
Its target is about two percent inflation a year. Not zero. Why? The Fed says low, steady inflation helps families and businesses plan saving, borrowing and investing.
Pizza Math, super simplified: ten pizzas, ten dollars, one dollar each. Print ten more dollars, and pizza costs two. But bake ten more pizzas too, and it's one dollar again.
The trick is growing the money and the pizzas together, slowly.
In June 2022, prices were up nine point one percent. That's a lot of pizza.
[Visual: Fed building, Dummy with a thermometer at "2%", PIZZA MATH stamp, then a pizza with "9.1%" sweating.]

## 5. YouTube description and pinned comment

Why can't the government just print more money and make everyone rich? In 2009, Zimbabwe launched a one-hundred-trillion-dollar banknote, and on the black market it was worth about thirty U.S. dollars. In 1923, Germans used banknotes as wallpaper.

We follow the money from Weimar Germany to Zimbabwe to the U.S. in 2020–2022, when the money supply grew almost 40% and inflation hit 9.1%, the highest since 1981. Plus: Pizza Math, the simplest way to see why more money without more stuff just means higher prices.

Inflation isn't only about printing: it also depends on how much the economy produces, and a little inflation is normal (the Fed aims for about 2%). The pizza example is a simplified illustration, not a model of the real economy. This video is for education, not financial advice.
