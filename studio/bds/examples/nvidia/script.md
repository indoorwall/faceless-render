## Guion final (12 escenas, voz EN-A)

**1.** Last Thursday morning, Nvidia lost about six hundred billion dollars in value. Right after the opening bell. That's more than fifteen hundred dollars for every single person in America.

**2.** But here's the strange part. By the end of the day, more than four hundred billion of it... was back. Nobody robbed a vault. Nobody returned any cash. So where did all that money go? And where did it come back from?

**3.** To answer that, we need to go back to a Denny's in San Jose, California. In the early nineties, three engineers met there and decided to start a chip company. One of them was Jensen Huang. They called it Nvidia. Their chips started out making video games look good. Three decades later, they power almost every A.I. tool you've ever used. In 2024, Nvidia briefly became the most valuable company in the world.

**4.** And for thirty-three years, one man ran it. Jensen Huang, the guy in the black leather jacket. To a lot of investors, Huang wasn't just the boss. He was Nvidia. Wall Street has a name for that: key-person risk. It's the risk that a company depends so much on one person that, if they go, the whole thing wobbles.

**5.** Now, to be fair, Nvidia wasn't having a great fall. Its stock was already down about a fifth from its October peak. Then came the week that changed everything.

**6.** On Sunday, Huang told employees he'd had an affair. That alone might have stayed a private story. But news reports said U.S. officials had national security concerns about the person involved. Nvidia's independent directors opened an inquiry.

**7.** Monday: the stock fell four percent. Tuesday: another five. The board hired a powerful law firm, led by a former federal prosecutor, and said it had no reason to believe company secrets were compromised. Investors weren't so sure.

**8.** On Wednesday, the stock actually bounced back a little. Then that night, The New York Times reported a second relationship, this time with an Nvidia employee. The board met. And by the end of the night, Jensen Huang was out. Resigned, effective immediately. Thursday morning, Nvidia made it official. Its finance chief, Colette Kress, became interim CEO. And when the market opened, the stock plunged as much as fourteen percent.

**9.** So, back to our mystery. Where did six hundred billion dollars go in a single morning? Here's the twist: it was never sitting anywhere as cash.

**10.** Picture Nvidia as a giant pizza, cut into about twenty-four billion slices. Each slice is a share. And here's the key: the price of a slice isn't set by the chef. It's set by whatever the last buyer paid. Wednesday night, the last slices sold for about a hundred and seventy-four dollars. Thursday morning, scared owners rushed to sell. To find buyers fast, they accepted less... and less... down to about a hundred and forty-nine. Same pizza. Same chips. Same factories. Same twenty-four billion slices. Just a lower price, multiplied by every slice. That's your six hundred billion dollars.

**11.** And then the mood shifted. Bargain hunters jumped in. According to Vanda Research, small investors poured more money into Nvidia in the first thirty minutes than on any opening on record. Buyers paid more, the price climbed, and by the closing bell most of the drop was gone. If you sold at the bottom, that loss was very real. For everyone else, it was a number on a screen. And if you own an index fund, you probably own a tiny bit of Nvidia. But it's one slice of a much bigger pie.

**12.** So here's what Nvidia's wild week really teaches us. A company's price isn't just about what it makes. It's about how people feel about it. On Thursday morning, the factories didn't change. The chips didn't change. The mood did. This is The Money Slice. We make money make sense. Next time: the six-billion-dollar crypto heist.
